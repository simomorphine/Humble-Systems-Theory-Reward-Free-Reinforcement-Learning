# Chapter 3 — The Potential-Difference Form and Its Consequences


## 3.1 The telescoping identity

The potential-difference form of debt has a structural consequence for discounted sums: the imaginary part of the Q-value telescopes to a closed-form expression involving the potential and a discounted average of future potentials.

**Setting 3.1.1.** Let $\mathcal{B}$ be a belief space, $\mathcal{A}$ an action space, $p(\cdot \mid b, a)$ a transition kernel, and $\pi : \mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{A}$ a deterministic policy. Let $\lambda \in [0, 1)$. Let $d : \mathcal{B} \times \mathcal{B} \to \mathbb{R}$ take the potential-difference form $d(b, b') = \phi(b') - \phi(b)$ for some $\phi : \mathcal{B} \to \mathbb{R}$.

**Definition 3.1.2 (Imaginary Q-value).** The *imaginary Q-value* under $\pi$ is
$$Q_I^\pi(b, a) = \mathbb{E}^\pi\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \, d(B_{t+k}, B_{t+k+1}) \,\middle|\, B_t = b, A_t = a\right],$$
where the expectation is over trajectories generated by $\pi$ and $p$.

**Theorem 3.1.3 (Telescoping identity).** Under Setting 3.1.1,
$$Q_I^\pi(b, a) = -\phi(b) + (1 - \lambda) \, \mathbb{E}^\pi\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \, \phi(B_{t+k+1}) \,\middle|\, B_t = b, A_t = a\right].$$

*Proof.* Substitute $d(B_{t+k}, B_{t+k+1}) = \phi(B_{t+k+1}) - \phi(B_{t+k})$:
$$Q_I^\pi(b, a) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \phi(B_{t+k+1}) - \sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \phi(B_{t+k})\right].$$

Reindex the first sum with $j = k + 1$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \phi(B_{t+k+1}) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda^{j-1} \phi(B_{t+j}).$$

Split the second sum at $k = 0$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \phi(B_{t+k}) = \phi(B_t) + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda^k \phi(B_{t+k}).$$

Combining:
$$Q_I^\pi = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^\infty (\lambda^{k-1} - \lambda^k) \phi(B_{t+k}) - \phi(B_t)\right] = -\phi(b) + (1 - \lambda) \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda^{k-1} \phi(B_{t+k})\right].$$

Reindexing $j = k - 1$ gives the stated form. $\square$

**Remark 3.1.4 (The identity is algebraic).** The telescoping identity holds for *any* potential $\phi$. It does not depend on any semantic interpretation of $\phi$. It is a purely algebraic fact about potential differences and discounted sums.

**Corollary 3.1.5 (Reduction to a discounted average).** Define the *discounted future potential average*
$$\bar{\phi}^\pi(b, a) = (1 - \lambda) \, \mathbb{E}^\pi\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \, \phi(B_{t+k+1}) \,\middle|\, B_t = b, A_t = a\right].$$
Then
$$Q_I^\pi(b, a) = \bar{\phi}^\pi(b, a) - \phi(b).$$

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 3.1.3. $\square$

**Remark 3.1.6 (Interpretation).** The imaginary Q-value is the difference between the discounted future average of the potential and the current potential. Under the information-theoretic reading (Section 3.3), this is the discounted cumulative information gain. Under the physical reading, it is the discounted change in mechanical potential. The algebra is the same; the interpretation differs.

## 3.2 Non-negativity

The imaginary Q-value is non-negative under an additional assumption on the potential.

**Definition 3.2.1 (Submartingale condition).** The potential $\phi$ satisfies the *submartingale condition* under policy $\pi$ if, for all $(b, a)$,
$$\mathbb{E}^\pi[\phi(B_{t+1}) \mid B_t = b, A_t = a] \ge \phi(b).$$

**Proposition 3.2.2 (Non-negativity of $Q_I^\pi$).** Under the submartingale condition, $Q_I^\pi(b, a) \ge 0$ for all $(b, a)$.

*Proof.* By the submartingale condition, the process $\lbrace \phi(B_{t+k})\rbrace_{k \ge 0}$ is a submartingale along any trajectory under $\pi$. Iterating the submartingale inequality,

$$\mathbb{E}^\pi[\phi(B_{t+k}) \mid B_t = b, A_t = a] \ge \phi(b)$$

for all $k \ge 0$. Therefore
$$\bar{\phi}^\pi(b, a) = (1-\lambda) \sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \mathbb{E}^\pi[\phi(B_{t+k+1}) \mid B_t = b, A_t = a] \ge (1-\lambda) \sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \phi(b) = \phi(b).$$
Subtracting $\phi(b)$ gives $Q_I^\pi(b, a) \ge 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.2.3 (Bayesian learning satisfies the submartingale condition).** If $\phi = -H(\Theta \mid \cdot)$ for a latent variable $\Theta$, and the belief is updated by Bayes' rule, then the submartingale condition holds.

*Proof.* Bayesian updates never increase conditional entropy on average: $H(\Theta \mid B_{t+1}) \le H(\Theta \mid B_t)$ in expectation. Hence $\phi = -H$ is a submartingale. $\square$

**Remark 3.2.4 (Boundedness is sufficient).** If $\phi$ is bounded, then $Q_I^\pi$ is bounded by $2 \sup |\phi|$ (see Proposition 6.2.5 for the exact statement). Boundedness plus the submartingale condition gives a bounded, non-negative imaginary Q-value.

## 3.3 The mutual-information interpretation

The telescoping identity gives a closed form for $Q_I^\pi$. Under a semantic assumption on $\phi$, this closed form has an information-theoretic reading.

**Assumption 3.3.1 (Semantic interpretation).** There exists a latent variable $\Theta$ taking values in a measurable space $(\Theta, \mathcal{T})$, such that
$$\phi(b) = -H(\Theta \mid B_t = b),$$
where $H$ denotes conditional entropy, and the belief state $b$ is a sufficient statistic for $\Theta$ given the history.

**Remark 3.3.2 (Status of the assumption).** Assumption 3.3.1 is *semantic*, not mathematical. The algebra of the telescoping identity holds for any $\phi$. The identification with mutual information requires $\phi = -H(\Theta \mid \cdot)$. The framework does not derive the semantic assumption; it uses it. This point is fundamental and is restated in Part V.

**Proposition 3.3.3 (One-step imaginary utility equals mutual information).** Under Assumption 3.3.1 and the Markov property,
$$\mathbb{E}^\pi[d(b, b') \mid B_t = b, A_t = a] = I(B_{t+1}; \Theta \mid B_t = b, A_t = a),$$
where $I$ denotes conditional mutual information.

*Proof.* Compute:
$$\mathbb{E}[d(b, B_{t+1})] = \mathbb{E}[\phi(B_{t+1})] - \phi(b) = -\mathbb{E}[H(\Theta \mid B_{t+1})] + H(\Theta \mid B_t = b).$$
By the chain rule for entropy,
$$H(\Theta \mid B_t) - H(\Theta \mid B_{t+1}, B_t) = I(\Theta; B_{t+1} \mid B_t).$$
Under the Markov property (the belief $B_{t+1}$ is a sufficient statistic for $\Theta$ given $B_t, A_t$), $H(\Theta \mid B_{t+1}, B_t) = H(\Theta \mid B_{t+1})$. Hence the expression equals $I(\Theta; B_{t+1} \mid B_t = b, A_t = a)$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.3.4 (Imaginary Q-value as discounted mutual information).** Under Assumption 3.3.1 and the Markov property,
$$Q_I^\pi(b, a) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \, I(B_{t+k+1}; \Theta \mid B_{t+k}, A_{t+k}),$$
where the expectation is under $\pi$ with $B_t = b$, $A_t = a$.

*Proof.* Combine Theorem 3.1.3 and Proposition 3.3.3. $\square$

**Remark 3.3.5 (Sign convention).** With $\phi = -H$, the debt $d = \phi(b') - \phi(b) = H(\Theta \mid b) - H(\Theta \mid b')$ is the reduction in uncertainty. This is non-negative when the transition reduces uncertainty, which is the typical case. The sign convention is consistent: positive debt = information gain.

**Remark 3.3.6 (What the interpretation does and does not give).** Under Assumption 3.3.1:

- The imaginary Q-value is *discounted cumulative information gain*.
- The exploration signal is the variance of the imaginary advantage (Chapter 10).
- The HST Equilibrium Axiom becomes: the system converges to a state of zero information gain (Chapter 13).

Without Assumption 3.3.1, the algebra holds, but the interpretation as information gain does not. The framework is honest about this: the mutual-information reading is a *reading*, not a derivation.

## 3.4 The real part

The real part of the Q-value satisfies an analogous but distinct equation.

**Proposition 3.4.1 (Real Q-value is discounted cumulative cost).** Under a deterministic policy $\pi$,
$$Q_R^\pi(b, a) = \mathbb{E}^\pi\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \, c(B_{t+k}, B_{t+k+1}) \,\middle|\, B_t = b, A_t = a\right].$$

*Proof.* The real part of the Bellman equation is
$$Q_R^\pi(b, a) = \mathbb{E}[c(b, b') + \lambda Q_R^\pi(b', \pi(b'))].$$
Iterating gives the stated form. $\square$

**Proposition 3.4.2 (No telescoping for the real part).** The real Q-value does not telescope to a closed form in general.

*Proof.* If $c$ were a potential difference, it would be antisymmetric and cycle-invariant. The cost $c$ is non-negative and is not assumed antisymmetric or cycle-invariant. Hence no telescoping in general. $\square$

**Remark 3.4.3 (Structural asymmetry of the framework).** The real part of the Q-value is path-dependent and irreversible; the imaginary part is path-independent and reversible. This distinction is the structural core of the framework, and it is what justifies treating the two components as parts of a complex number rather than as two independent real numbers.

## 3.5 The complex Bellman equation

Combining the real and imaginary components gives the complex Bellman equation.

**Definition 3.5.1 (Complex evaluation operator).** For a deterministic policy $\pi$, the *complex evaluation operator* $T^\pi : \mathcal{Q} \to \mathcal{Q}$ is
$$(T^\pi Q)(b, a) = \mathbb{E}_{b' \sim p(\cdot \mid b, a)}\left[z(b, a, b') + \lambda \, Q(b', \pi(b'))\right],$$
where $z(b, a, b') = c(b, a, b') + i\, d(b, a, b')$ and $\mathcal{Q} = \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{B} \times \mathcal{A}, \mathbb{C})$ is the Banach space of bounded complex functions with the sup-norm.

**Theorem 3.5.2 (Evaluation contraction).** $T^\pi$ is a $\lambda$-contraction on $(\mathcal{Q}, \|\cdot\|_\infty)$.

*Proof.* For $Q_1, Q_2 \in \mathcal{Q}$ and any $(b, a)$:

$$|(T^\pi Q_1)(b, a) - (T^\pi Q_2)(b, a)| = \lambda \left|\mathbb{E}_{b'}[Q_1(b', \pi(b')) - Q_2(b', \pi(b'))]\right| \le \lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{b'}[|Q_1(b', \pi(b')) - Q_2(b', \pi(b'))|] \le \lambda \, \|Q_1 - Q_2\|_\infty.$$

The first inequality uses Proposition 2.3.2 (modulus of expectation); the second uses the definition of the sup-norm. Taking the supremum over $(b, a)$ gives the contraction. $\square$

**Corollary 3.5.3 (Existence and uniqueness of $Q^\pi$).** For any deterministic policy $\pi$, there exists a unique $Q^\pi \in \mathcal{Q}$ satisfying $T^\pi Q^\pi = Q^\pi$. Moreover, $Q^\pi = \lim_{n \to \infty} (T^\pi)^n Q_0$ for any $Q_0 \in \mathcal{Q}$.

*Proof.* Banach fixed-point theorem applied to $T^\pi$ on the complete space $(\mathcal{Q}, \|\cdot\|_\infty)$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.5.4 (Bound on the fixed point).** $\mid Q^\pi \mid_\infty \le Z_{\max}/(1-\lambda)$, where $Z_{\max} = \sup_{b, a, b'} |z(b, a, b')|$.

*Proof.* $|Q^\pi(b, a)| = |T^\pi Q^\pi(b, a)| \le Z_{\max} + \lambda \|Q^\pi\|_\infty$. Rearranging gives the bound. $\square$

**Corollary 3.5.5 (Component equations).** The real and imaginary parts of $Q^\pi$ satisfy separate Bellman equations:

$$Q_R^\pi(b, a) = \mathbb{E}_{b'}[c(b, a, b') + \lambda Q_R^\pi(b', \pi(b'))],$$

$$Q_I^\pi(b, a) = \mathbb{E}_{b'}[d(b, a, b') + \lambda Q_I^\pi(b', \pi(b'))].$$

*Proof.* Taking real and imaginary parts of the complex Bellman equation. $\square$

## 3.6 Summary

Three results have been established.

**First, the telescoping identity (Theorem 3.1.3):** for any potential-difference debt, the imaginary Q-value has a closed form involving the potential and a discounted future average. The identity is algebraic and holds for any potential.

**Second, non-negativity (Proposition 3.2.2):** under the submartingale condition, the imaginary Q-value is non-negative. The condition holds automatically under Bayesian learning with $\phi = -H$.

**Third, the mutual-information interpretation (Corollary 3.3.4):** under the semantic assumption $\phi = -H(\Theta \mid \cdot)$, the imaginary Q-value equals discounted cumulative mutual information. The interpretation is conditional on the semantic assumption; it is not derived from the algebra.

The real part has no telescoping, because the cost is not a potential difference. The separation of real and imaginary parts is complete in evaluation: each satisfies its own Bellman equation and can be computed independently.

The complex evaluation operator is a $\lambda$-contraction. This is the first convergence result of the book, and it is the foundation for the more difficult optimality results in Part III.
