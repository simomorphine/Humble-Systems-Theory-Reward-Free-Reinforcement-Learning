# Chapter 3 — The Potential-Difference Form and Its Consequences

## 3.1 The telescoping identity

The potential-difference form of debt has a structural consequence for discounted sums: the imaginary part of the Q-value telescopes to a closed-form expression involving the potential and a discounted average of future potentials.

**Setting 3.1.1.** Let \(\mathcal{B}\) be a belief space, \(\mathcal{A}\) an action space, \(p(\cdot \mid b,a)\) a transition kernel, and \(\pi:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}\) a deterministic policy. Let \(\lambda\in[0,1)\). Let

$$
d:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
$$

take the potential-difference form

$$
d(b,b')=\phi(b')-\phi(b)
$$

for some potential \(\phi:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}\).

**Definition 3.1.2 (Imaginary Q-value).** The *imaginary Q-value* under \(\pi\) is

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k d(B_{t+k},B_{t+k+1})
\,\middle|\,
B_t=b,A_t=a
\right],
$$

where the expectation is over trajectories generated by \(\pi\) and \(p\).

**Theorem 3.1.3 (Telescoping identity).** Under Setting 3.1.1,

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
-\phi(b)
+
(1-\lambda)
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k
\phi(B_{t+k+1})
\,\middle|\,
B_t=b,A_t=a
\right].
$$

*Proof.* Substitute

$$
d(B_{t+k},B_{t+k+1})
=
\phi(B_{t+k+1})-\phi(B_{t+k}).
$$

Then

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k\phi(B_{t+k+1})
-
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k\phi(B_{t+k})
\right].
$$

Reindexing the first sum with \(j=k+1\),

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k\phi(B_{t+k+1})
=
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}
\lambda^{j-1}\phi(B_{t+j}).
$$

Also,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k\phi(B_{t+k})
=
\phi(B_t)
+
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
\lambda^k\phi(B_{t+k}).
$$

Therefore,

$$
Q_I^\pi
=
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
-\phi(B_t)
+
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
(\lambda^{k-1}-\lambda^k)\phi(B_{t+k})
\right].
$$

Since \(B_t=b\),

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
-\phi(b)
+
(1-\lambda)
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
\lambda^{k-1}\phi(B_{t+k})
\right].
$$

Reindexing with \(j=k-1\) gives the stated expression. \(\square\)

**Remark 3.1.4 (The identity is algebraic).** The telescoping identity holds for any potential \(\phi\). It does not depend on any semantic interpretation of \(\phi\). It is a purely algebraic fact about potential differences and discounted sums.

**Corollary 3.1.5 (Reduction to a discounted average).** Define the *discounted future potential average*

$$
\bar{\phi}^\pi(b,a)
=
(1-\lambda)
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k\phi(B_{t+k+1})
\,\middle|\,
B_t=b,A_t=a
\right].
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
\bar{\phi}^\pi(b,a)-\phi(b).
}
$$

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 3.1.3. \(\square\)

**Remark 3.1.6 (Interpretation).** The imaginary Q-value is the difference between the discounted future average of the potential and the current potential. The algebraic identity itself is interpretation-independent. Under the information-theoretic interpretation introduced in Section 3.3, this difference corresponds to discounted cumulative information change.

## 3.2 Non-negativity

The sign of the imaginary Q-value depends on the direction in which the potential changes on average.

**Definition 3.2.1 (Submartingale condition).** The potential \(\phi\) satisfies the *submartingale condition* under policy \(\pi\) if, for all \((b,a)\),

$$
\mathbb{E}^\pi
[
\phi(B_{t+1})
\mid
B_t=b,A_t=a
]
\geq
\phi(b).
$$

**Proposition 3.2.2 (Non-negativity of \(Q_I^\pi\)).** Under the submartingale condition,

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)\geq0
$$

for all \((b,a)\).

*Proof.* Iterating the submartingale inequality gives

$$
\mathbb{E}^\pi
[
\phi(B_{t+k})
\mid
B_t=b,A_t=a
]
\geq
\phi(b)
$$

for all \(k\geq0\). Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{\phi}^\pi(b,a)
&=
(1-\lambda)
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k
\mathbb{E}^\pi
[
\phi(B_{t+k+1})
\mid B_t=b,A_t=a
]\\
&\geq
(1-\lambda)
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k\phi(b)\\
&=\phi(b).
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence,

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
\bar{\phi}^\pi(b,a)-\phi(b)
\geq0.
$$

\(\square\)

**Remark 3.2.3 (Sign convention).** The sign of \(Q_I^\pi\) follows the direction of the potential:

* if the potential increases on average, then \(Q_I^\pi\geq0\);
* if the potential is a martingale, then \(Q_I^\pi=0\);
* if the potential decreases on average, then \(Q_I^\pi\leq0\).

In the information-theoretic interpretation below, we choose the potential so that

$$
\boxed{
\text{negative debt}=\text{information gain}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\text{positive debt}=\text{information loss}.
}
$$

This convention will determine the sign of the entropy potential.

**Remark 3.2.4 (Boundedness).** If \(\phi\) is bounded, then \(Q_I^\pi\) is bounded. In particular,

$$
|Q_I^\pi(b,a)|
\leq
\frac{2\|\phi\|_\infty}{1}
$$

from the potential-difference representation, and sharper bounds can be obtained from the discounted-average representation. See Proposition 6.2.5 for the exact statement.

## 3.3 The mutual-information interpretation

The telescoping identity gives a closed form for \(Q_I^\pi\). Under a semantic assumption on \(\phi\), this closed form acquires an information-theoretic interpretation.

**Assumption 3.3.1 (Semantic interpretation).** There exists a latent variable \(\Theta\) taking values in a measurable space \((\Theta,\mathcal{T})\), such that

$$
\boxed{
\phi(b)=H(\Theta\mid B_t=b),
}
$$

where \(H\) denotes conditional entropy, and the belief state \(b\) is a sufficient statistic for \(\Theta\) given the history.

The choice

$$
\phi=H
$$

is made deliberately to preserve the convention

$$
\boxed{
\text{negative debt}=\text{information gain}.
}
$$

Indeed, if a transition reduces uncertainty, then entropy decreases and the corresponding debt is negative.

**Remark 3.3.2 (Status of the assumption).** Assumption 3.3.1 is *semantic*, not mathematical. The algebra of the telescoping identity holds for any \(\phi\). The identification with mutual information requires the additional choice

$$
\phi=H(\Theta\mid\cdot).
$$

The framework does not derive this semantic identification; it uses it.

**Proposition 3.3.3 (One-step imaginary utility equals negative mutual information).** Under Assumption 3.3.1 and the Markov/sufficiency condition,

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}^\pi
[
d(B_t,B_{t+1})
\mid
B_t=b,A_t=a
]
=
-
I(\Theta;B_{t+1}\mid B_t=b,A_t=a).
}
$$

*Proof.* Since

$$
d(B_t,B_{t+1})
=
H(\Theta\mid B_{t+1})
-
H(\Theta\mid B_t),
$$

we obtain

$$
\mathbb{E}[d(B_t,B_{t+1})]
=
\mathbb{E}[H(\Theta\mid B_{t+1})]
-
H(\Theta\mid B_t).
$$

By the conditional mutual-information identity,

$$
I(\Theta;B_{t+1}\mid B_t,A_t)
=
H(\Theta\mid B_t,A_t)
-
H(\Theta\mid B_{t+1},B_t,A_t).
$$

Under the stated sufficiency/Markov condition, the relevant conditional entropy after the update is represented by \(H(\Theta\mid B_{t+1})\). Hence

$$
\mathbb{E}[d(B_t,B_{t+1})]
=
-
I(\Theta;B_{t+1}\mid B_t,A_t).
$$

Conditioning on \(B_t=b,A_t=a\) gives the result. \(\square\)

**Corollary 3.3.4 (Imaginary Q-value as negative discounted mutual information).** Under Assumption 3.3.1 and the Markov/sufficiency condition,

$$
\boxed{
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
-
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k
I(
\Theta;B_{t+k+1}
\mid
B_{t+k},A_{t+k}
)
\,\middle|\,
B_t=b,A_t=a
\right].
}
$$

Thus \(Q_I^\pi\leq0\) whenever the information gains are non-negative.

*Proof.* Apply Proposition 3.3.3 at every step and use linearity of expectation. \(\square\)

**Remark 3.3.5 (Sign convention).** With

$$
\phi=H(\Theta\mid\cdot),
$$

the debt is

$$
d(b,b')
=
H(\Theta\mid b')
-
H(\Theta\mid b).
$$

Therefore:

* uncertainty reduction gives \(d<0\);
* no expected change in uncertainty gives \(d=0\);
* uncertainty increase gives \(d>0\).

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
\text{negative debt = information gain}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\text{positive debt = information loss}.
}
$$

This is the sign convention adopted throughout the framework.

**Remark 3.3.6 (What the interpretation does and does not give).** Under Assumption 3.3.1:

* the imaginary Q-value is **negative discounted cumulative information gain**;
* equivalently, \(-Q_I^\pi\) is discounted cumulative mutual information;
* the exploration signal can be defined from the variance of the imaginary advantage (Chapter 10);
* an equilibrium condition in which the expected information gain vanishes corresponds to \(Q_I^\pi=0\) at the relevant equilibrium.

Without Assumption 3.3.1, the algebra holds, but the interpretation in terms of information gain does not. The framework therefore treats the mutual-information reading as a **semantic interpretation**, not as a consequence derived from the potential-difference algebra alone.

## 3.4 The real part

The real part of the Q-value satisfies an analogous but structurally distinct equation.

**Proposition 3.4.1 (Real Q-value is discounted cumulative cost).** Under a deterministic policy \(\pi\),

$$
Q_R^\pi(b,a)
=
\mathbb{E}^\pi
\left[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\lambda^k
c(B_{t+k},B_{t+k+1})
\,\middle|\,
B_t=b,A_t=a
\right].
$$

*Proof.* The real part of the Bellman equation is

$$
Q_R^\pi(b,a)
=
\mathbb{E}
[
c(b,b')
+
\lambda Q_R^\pi(b',\pi(b'))
].
$$

Iterating gives the stated discounted sum. \(\square\)

**Remark 3.4.2 (Structural asymmetry of the framework).** The real part is generally path-dependent because cost accumulates along transitions. The imaginary part has additional structure when the debt is a potential difference: its cumulative sum can be reduced to boundary terms through telescoping.

Thus the distinction between the two components is not merely that one is called "real" and the other "imaginary." Their mathematical structures are different:

$$
\boxed{
\text{cost: generally path-dependent}
}
$$

whereas

$$
\boxed{
\text{potential-difference debt: telescoping}.
}
$$

## 3.5 The complex Bellman equation

Combining the real and imaginary components gives the complex Bellman equation.

**Definition 3.5.1 (Complex evaluation operator).** For a deterministic policy \(\pi\), the *complex evaluation operator*

$$
T^\pi:\mathcal Q\to\mathcal Q
$$

is defined by

$$
(T^\pi Q)(b,a)
=
\mathbb{E}_{b'\sim p(\cdot\mid b,a)}
\left[
z(b,a,b')
+
\lambda Q(b',\pi(b'))
\right],
$$

where

$$
z(b,a,b')
=
c(b,a,b')
+
i\,d(b,a,b')
$$

and

$$
\mathcal Q
=
\mathcal B(\mathcal B\times\mathcal A,\mathbb C)
$$

is the Banach space of bounded complex-valued functions equipped with the sup-norm

$$
\|Q\|_\infty
=
\sup_{b,a}|Q(b,a)|.
$$

**Theorem 3.5.2 (Evaluation contraction).** \(T^\pi\) is a \(\lambda\)-contraction on \((\mathcal Q,\|\cdot\|_\infty)\).

*Proof.* For \(Q_1,Q_2\in\mathcal Q\),

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
|(T^\pi Q_1)(b,a)-(T^\pi Q_2)(b,a)|
\\
&=
\lambda
\left|
\mathbb E_{b'}
[
Q_1(b',\pi(b'))
-
Q_2(b',\pi(b'))
]
\right|
\\
&\leq
\lambda
\mathbb E_{b'}
\left[
|Q_1(b',\pi(b'))
-
Q_2(b',\pi(b'))|
\right]
\\
&\leq
\lambda\|Q_1-Q_2\|_\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first inequality uses the modulus-of-expectation inequality. Taking the supremum over \((b,a)\) gives

$$
\boxed{
\|T^\pi Q_1-T^\pi Q_2\|_\infty
\leq
\lambda\|Q_1-Q_2\|_\infty.
}
$$

Hence \(T^\pi\) is a \(\lambda\)-contraction. \(\square\)

**Corollary 3.5.3 (Existence and uniqueness of \(Q^\pi\)).** For any deterministic policy \(\pi\), there exists a unique

$$
Q^\pi\in\mathcal Q
$$

satisfying

$$
T^\pi Q^\pi=Q^\pi.
$$

Moreover,

$$
Q^\pi
=
\lim_{n\to\infty}(T^\pi)^nQ_0
$$

for any \(Q_0\in\mathcal Q\).

*Proof.* This follows from the Banach fixed-point theorem applied to \(T^\pi\) on the complete space \((\mathcal Q,\|\cdot\|_\infty)\). \(\square\)

**Corollary 3.5.4 (Bound on the fixed point).** Let

$$
Z_{\max}
=
\sup_{b,a,b'}|z(b,a,b')|.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
\|Q^\pi\|_\infty
\leq
\frac{Z_{\max}}{1-\lambda}.
}
$$

*Proof.* Since \(Q^\pi=T^\pi Q^\pi\),

$$
|Q^\pi(b,a)|
\leq
Z_{\max}
+
\lambda\|Q^\pi\|_\infty.
$$

Taking the supremum and rearranging gives the result. \(\square\)

**Corollary 3.5.5 (Component equations).** The real and imaginary parts of \(Q^\pi\) satisfy separate Bellman equations:

$$
Q_R^\pi(b,a)
=
\mathbb E_{b'}
[
c(b,a,b')
+
\lambda Q_R^\pi(b',\pi(b'))
],
$$

and

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)
=
\mathbb E_{b'}
[
d(b,a,b')
+
\lambda Q_I^\pi(b',\pi(b'))
].
$$

*Proof.* Taking real and imaginary parts of the complex Bellman equation. \(\square\)

## 3.6 Summary

Three main results have been established.

**First, the telescoping identity (Theorem 3.1.3).** For any potential-difference debt,

$$
d(b,b')=\phi(b')-\phi(b),
$$

the imaginary Q-value admits a closed form involving the current potential and a discounted future potential average. This identity is purely algebraic and holds for any potential.

**Second, the sign of the imaginary Q-value depends on the potential dynamics.** A submartingale potential gives

$$
Q_I^\pi\geq0,
$$

a martingale gives

$$
Q_I^\pi=0,
$$

and a supermartingale gives

$$
Q_I^\pi\leq0.
$$

**Third, the information-theoretic interpretation requires a semantic choice.** To preserve the convention

$$
\boxed{
\text{negative debt}=\text{information gain},
\qquad
\text{positive debt}=\text{information loss},
}
$$

we choose

$$
\boxed{
\phi(b)=H(\Theta\mid B_t=b).
}
$$

Then

$$
d(b,b')
=
H(\Theta\mid b')-H(\Theta\mid b),
$$

so an uncertainty reduction produces negative debt. Moreover,

$$
Q_I^\pi
=
-\text{discounted cumulative mutual information}.
$$

Thus \(-Q_I^\pi\), rather than \(Q_I^\pi\) itself, measures discounted cumulative information gain under this convention.

The real part has no analogous telescoping property in general because the cost need not be a potential difference. It remains a discounted cumulative path-dependent quantity.

Finally, the complex evaluation operator is a \(\lambda\)-contraction in the sup-norm. This gives existence and uniqueness of the policy-evaluation fixed point and provides the foundation for the more difficult optimality results in Part III.

Compute \(Q_I^\pi(b_1,a)\) for the unique deterministic policy.

**Exercise 3.2.** For the same cMDP, compute \(\bar\phi^\pi(b_1,a)\) and verify that

$$
Q_I^\pi(b_1,a)
=
\bar\phi^\pi(b_1,a)-\phi(b_1).
$$

**Exercise 3.3.** Let \(\phi(b)=-b^2\) on \(\mathcal B=\mathbb R\), and let the dynamics be a random walk with

$$
B_{t+1}=B_t+\epsilon,
$$

where \(\epsilon\) is a symmetric random variable with mean zero. Show that \(\phi\) is a supermartingale along any trajectory. Compute \(Q_I^\pi(b,a)\).

**Exercise 3.4.** Let \(\phi(b)=b\) on \(\mathcal B=\mathbb R\), and let the dynamics be a random walk with

$$
B_{t+1}=B_t+\epsilon,
\qquad
\mathbb E[\epsilon]=0.
$$

Is \(\phi\) a submartingale? A martingale? Compute \(Q_I^\pi(b,a)\).

**Exercise 3.5.** Prove that if \(\phi\) is a martingale, i.e.

$$
\mathbb E[\phi(B_{t+1})\mid B_t]=\phi(B_t),
$$

then

$$
Q_I^\pi(b,a)=0
$$

for all \((b,a)\).

**Exercise 3.6.** Give an example of a potential \(\phi\) that is not a submartingale, and compute \(Q_I^\pi\) for a simple cMDP to show that it can be negative.

**Exercise 3.7.** Suppose

$$
\phi=H(\Theta\mid\cdot)
$$

and the belief is updated by Bayes' rule. Prove that

$$
\mathbb E[\phi(B_{t+1})\mid B_t]
\leq
\phi(B_t),
$$

with equality if and only if the observation provides no additional information about \(\Theta\).

**Exercise 3.8 (Discussion).** The mutual-information interpretation of \(Q_I^\pi\) requires Assumption 3.3.1. Is this assumption reasonable for all information processing systems? Give an example of a system for which the assumption might fail, and discuss what the framework would say about that system.

**Exercise 3.9.** Prove that if \(c\) is a potential difference, then \(c\) must be antisymmetric and cycle-invariant. Conclude that a non-negative cost cannot be a potential difference unless it is identically zero.

**Exercise 3.10.** Verify the evaluation contraction (Theorem 3.5.2) for a specific cMDP with two states and two actions. Choose \(Q_1,Q_2\in\mathcal Q\) and compute both sides of the contraction inequality.

**Exercise 3.11.** Suppose

$$
\|Q_1-Q_2\|_\infty=1.
$$

Give an example where

$$
\|T^\pi Q_1-T^\pi Q_2\|_\infty=\lambda
$$

and an example where it is strictly less than \(\lambda\).

**Exercise 3.12.** Show that the bound

$$
\|Q^\pi\|_\infty
\leq

$$

is tight by constructing a cMDP where

$$
|Q^\pi(b,a)|
=
\frac{Z_{\max}}{1-\lambda}
$$

for some \((b,a)\).

**Exercise 3.13 (Open).** The real part of the Q-value does not telescope. But what if the cost function has additional structure — e.g. it is a sum of a potential difference and a constant? Does the real part admit a partial telescoping? Characterize the cost functions for which the real part has a closed form.

**Exercise 3.14 (Open).** The telescoping identity holds for discounted sums with a single discount factor \(\lambda\). What happens if different components have different discount factors — e.g. \(\lambda_c\) for cost and \(\lambda_d\) for debt? Does the telescoping identity generalize? What is the resulting structure?
