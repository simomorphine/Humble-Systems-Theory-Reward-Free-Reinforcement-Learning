## 5.1 Asymmetry of the complex quasi-metric

**Definition 5.1.1 (Asymmetry).** The complex quasi-metric $Q$ is *symmetric* if $Q(b, b') = Q(b', b)$ for all $b, b' \in \mathcal{B}$. Otherwise it is *asymmetric*. The *asymmetry* at a pair $(b, b')$ is
$$\Delta Q(b, b') = Q(b, b') - Q(b', b) \in \mathbb{C}.$$

**Proposition 5.1.2 (Properties of the asymmetry).** For any $b, b'$:

- (i) $\Delta Q(b, b) = 0$.
- (ii) $\Delta Q(b, b') = -\Delta Q(b', b)$.
- (iii) If $d$ takes the potential-difference form, then
$$\operatorname{Im} \Delta Q(b, b') = 2(\phi(b') - \phi(b)).$$

*Proof.* (i) and (ii) are immediate from the definition. For (iii): $\operatorname{Im} \Delta Q = d(b, b') - d(b', b) = [\phi(b') - \phi(b)] - [\phi(b) - \phi(b')] = 2(\phi(b') - \phi(b))$. $\square$

**Remark 5.1.3 (Decomposition of the asymmetry).** The complex asymmetry decomposes into a real part and an imaginary part:
$$\Delta Q(b, b') = \Delta c(b, b') + 2i(\phi(b') - \phi(b)),$$
where $\Delta c(b, b') = c(b, b') - c(b', b)$ measures the energetic irreversibility of the transition. The imaginary part of the asymmetry is entirely determined by the potential.

**Remark 5.1.4 (Asymmetry is the generic case).** The framework does not assume symmetry. In a symmetric environment — where every transition is reversible at equal cost — the asymmetry vanishes and the framework reduces to a classical symmetric geometry. In an asymmetric environment — where some transitions are easier in one direction than the other — the asymmetry is non-zero, and the framework's distinctive structure emerges.

## 5.2 Forward and backward reachability

**Definition 5.2.1 (Forward and backward balls).** For $b \in \mathcal{B}$, $r > 0$, and $\gamma \in (0, 1]$:

- The *forward ball* is $B^+_\gamma(b, r) = \{b' \in \mathcal{B} : d_\gamma(b, b') < r\}$.
- The *backward ball* is $B^-_\gamma(b, r) = \{b' \in \mathcal{B} : d_\gamma(b', b) < r\}$.

**Proposition 5.2.2 (Forward and backward balls coincide iff symmetric).** For all $b, r$, $B^+_\gamma(b, r) = B^-_\gamma(b, r)$ if and only if $d_\gamma$ is symmetric.

*Proof.* ($\Leftarrow$) Immediate. ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $B^+_\gamma(b, r) = B^-_\gamma(b, r)$ for all $b, r$. Then for any $b' \in \mathcal{B}$, $b' \in B^+_\gamma(b, r)$ iff $b' \in B^-_\gamma(b, r)$, i.e. $d_\gamma(b, b') < r$ iff $d_\gamma(b', b) < r$ for all $r > 0$. This implies $d_\gamma(b, b') = d_\gamma(b', b)$ for all $b, b'$. $\square$

**Remark 5.2.3 (Asymmetry of the modulus vs. asymmetry of the argument).** The asymmetry of $Q$ implies asymmetry of $d_\gamma$ unless the asymmetry is confined to the argument: $Q(b, b') = e^{i\alpha} Q(b', b)$ for some phase $\alpha$. In that case $|Q(b, b')| = |Q(b', b)|$ and $d_1$ is symmetric even though $Q$ is not. The framework's asymmetry is primarily about the modulus; unless stated otherwise, "asymmetric" means $d_\gamma$ asymmetric for $\gamma > 0$.

## 5.3 The bitopological space

**Definition 5.3.1 (Forward topology).** The *forward topology* $\tau_+$ on $\mathcal{B}$ is the topology generated by the basis
$$\{B^+_\gamma(b, r) : b \in \mathcal{B}, r > 0\}.$$

**Definition 5.3.2 (Backward topology).** The *backward topology* $\tau_-$ on $\mathcal{B}$ is the topology generated by the basis
$$\{B^-_\gamma(b, r) : b \in \mathcal{B}, r > 0\}.$$

**Definition 5.3.3 (Bitopological space).** The triple $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_+, \tau_-)$ is a *bitopological space*. When we wish to indicate the topology being used, we write $(\mathcal{B}, \rightarrow)$ for $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_+)$ and $(\mathcal{B}, \leftarrow)$ for $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_-)$.

**Remark 5.3.4 (Historical note).** Bitopological spaces were introduced by Kelly (1963) in the context of quasi-uniform spaces and developed by Fletcher, Lindgren, Kopperman, and others. The construction here is the natural one arising from an asymmetric distance: the asymmetry gives two topologies, one for each direction of approach. The framework does not introduce a new mathematical structure; it applies an existing one to the geometry of complex cost.

**Proposition 5.3.5 (Independence of $\gamma$ for $\gamma > 0$).** For any $\gamma_1, \gamma_2 \in (0, 1]$, the forward topologies $\tau_+^{\gamma_1}$ and $\tau_+^{\gamma_2}$ coincide, and likewise for the backward topologies.

*Proof.* By Corollary 4.2.2, the quasi-metrics $d_{\gamma_1}$ and $d_{\gamma_2}$ are topologically equivalent. Hence they generate the same forward and backward topologies. $\square$

**Remark 5.3.6 (The $\gamma = 0$ case).** If $\gamma = 0$, the forward and backward balls are the same set $\{b' : c(b, b') < r\}$, and the two topologies coincide. The bitopological structure is trivial at $\gamma = 0$. This is another reason the framework emphasizes $\gamma > 0$.

## 5.4 Join, meet, and average topologies

**Definition 5.4.1 (Join topology).** The *join topology* $\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$ is the coarsest topology containing both $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$. A basis is given by
$$\{B^+_\gamma(b, r) \cap B^-_\gamma(b, s) : b \in \mathcal{B}, r, s > 0\}.$$

**Definition 5.4.2 (Meet topology).** The *meet topology* $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$ is the finest topology contained in both $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$. A basis is given by the unions of sets that are open in both topologies.

**Definition 5.4.3 (Average distance and topology).** The *average distance* is
$$d_{\text{avg}}(b, b') = \frac{1}{2}(d_\gamma(b, b') + d_\gamma(b', b)).$$
The *average topology* $\tau_{\text{avg}}$ is the topology generated by $d_{\text{avg}}$-balls.

**Proposition 5.4.4 (Properties of $d_{\text{avg}}$).**

- (i) $d_{\text{avg}}$ is symmetric: $d_{\text{avg}}(b, b') = d_{\text{avg}}(b', b)$.
- (ii) $d_{\text{avg}}$ satisfies the triangle inequality.
- (iii) $d_{\text{avg}}(b, b) = 0$.
- (iv) $d_{\text{avg}}(b, b') = 0$ if and only if $d_\gamma(b, b') = d_\gamma(b', b) = 0$.

*Proof.* (i) Immediate. (ii):
$$d_{\text{avg}}(b, b'') = \frac{1}{2}(d_\gamma(b, b'') + d_\gamma(b'', b)) \le \frac{1}{2}(d_\gamma(b, b') + d_\gamma(b', b'') + d_\gamma(b'', b') + d_\gamma(b', b)) = d_{\text{avg}}(b, b') + d_{\text{avg}}(b', b'').$$
(iii) Immediate. (iv) Immediate from non-negativity of $d_\gamma$. $\square$

**Remark 5.4.5.** $d_{\text{avg}}$ is a pseudometric on $\mathcal{B}$. It is a metric iff $d_\gamma(b, b') = 0$ implies $b = b'$. Throughout, we assume this separation property unless stated otherwise.

**Proposition 5.4.6 (Topological chain).**
$$\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- \subseteq \tau_{\text{avg}} \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-.$$

*Proof.* **Join inclusion.** A $d_{\text{avg}}$-ball $B_{\text{avg}}(b, r)$ contains the set $B^+_\gamma(b, r/2) \cap B^-_\gamma(b, r/2)$: if $b'$ is in the intersection, then $d_\gamma(b, b') < r/2$ and $d_\gamma(b', b) < r/2$, so $d_{\text{avg}}(b, b') < r/2 < r$. Hence every $d_{\text{avg}}$-ball is a union of sets open in the join topology, and $\tau_{\text{avg}} \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-$.

**Meet inclusion.** Suppose $U$ is open in both $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$. For each $b \in U$, there exist $r_+, r_- > 0$ such that $B^+_\gamma(b, r_+) \subseteq U$ and $B^-_\gamma(b, r_-) \subseteq U$. Let $r = \min(r_+, r_-)$. If $b' \in B_{\text{avg}}(b, r/2)$, then $d_{\text{avg}}(b, b') < r/2$, so $d_\gamma(b, b') < r$ and $d_\gamma(b', b) < r$. Hence $b' \in B^+_\gamma(b, r) \cap B^-_\gamma(b, r) \subseteq U$, so $B_{\text{avg}}(b, r/2) \subseteq U$. Thus $U$ is open in $\tau_{\text{avg}}$, and $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- \subseteq \tau_{\text{avg}}$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.4.7 (Chain restated).**
$$\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- \subseteq \tau_{\text{avg}} \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-, \qquad \tau_+ \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-, \qquad \tau_- \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-.$$

**Remark 5.4.8 (Interpretation).** The chain is the fundamental topological structure of the framework. The join topology is the *finest* (has the most open sets); the meet topology is the *coarsest*. The average sits in between. The three topologies correspond to three degrees of bitopological agreement:

- In $\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$, a set is open if it is simultaneously forward- and backward-open, or a union of such sets.
- In $\tau_{\text{avg}}$, a set is open if it is open for the symmetrized distance.
- In $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$, a set is open if it is open in both individual topologies.

The relationship between the three is the framework's analog of the relationship between the original topology and its symmetrization in the classical theory of quasi-metric spaces.

## 5.5 Closure and equilibrium sets

**Definition 5.5.1 (Pre-equilibrium set).** For a threshold function $\epsilon : \mathcal{B} \to (0, \infty)$, the *pre-equilibrium set* is
$$U_{\text{pre}} = \bigcup_{b \in \mathcal{B}} B_{\text{avg}}(b, \epsilon(b)).$$

**Definition 5.5.2 (Equilibrium sets).** For $\tau$ one of the topologies $\tau_+, \tau_-, \tau_{\text{avg}}, \tau_+ \vee \tau_-, \tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$, define
$$E_\tau = \overline{U_{\text{pre}}}^{\tau},$$
the closure of $U_{\text{pre}}$ in $\tau$.

**Theorem 5.5.3 (Equilibrium hierarchy).**
$$E_{\vee} \subseteq E_{\text{avg}} \subseteq E_{\wedge},$$
where $E_{\vee} = E_{\tau_+ \vee \tau_-}$, $E_{\text{avg}} = E_{\tau_{\text{avg}}}$, $E_{\wedge} = E_{\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-}$. Moreover,
$$E_{\vee} \subseteq E_{\rightarrow} \cap E_{\leftarrow},$$
where $E_{\rightarrow} = E_{\tau_+}$ and $E_{\leftarrow} = E_{\tau_-}$.

*Proof.* The closure operator is monotone with respect to topology inclusion in the *opposite* direction: if $\tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2$, then $\overline{A}^{\tau_2} \subseteq \overline{A}^{\tau_1}$ for every set $A$, because finer topologies have more closed sets, hence smaller closures. From Corollary 5.4.7:
$$\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- \subseteq \tau_{\text{avg}} \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-.$$
Taking closures of $U_{\text{pre}}$ in each topology:
$$\overline{U_{\text{pre}}}^{\tau_+ \vee \tau_-} \subseteq \overline{U_{\text{pre}}}^{\tau_{\text{avg}}} \subseteq \overline{U_{\text{pre}}}^{\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-},$$
i.e. $E_\vee \subseteq E_{\text{avg}} \subseteq E_\wedge$.

For the second statement: $\tau_+ \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-$ and $\tau_- \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-$, so $\overline{U_{\text{pre}}}^{\tau_+ \vee \tau_-} \subseteq \overline{U_{\text{pre}}}^{\tau_+}$ and similarly for $\tau_-$. Hence $E_\vee \subseteq E_\rightarrow \cap E_\leftarrow$. $\square$

**Remark 5.5.4 (Direction of inclusions).** The equilibrium hierarchy has the *join* topology giving the *smallest* equilibrium set and the *meet* topology giving the *largest*. This is the reverse of what a naïve reading might suggest. The reason is that the equilibrium set is defined via closure, and finer topologies give smaller closures. The join topology is the finest of the three (it has the most open sets), so its closure is smallest. The meet topology is the coarsest, so its closure is largest.

**Remark 5.5.5 (Correction from earlier drafts).** An earlier version of the framework had the inclusion $E_{\text{avg}} \subseteq E_\vee$ reversed. The correct direction is $E_\vee \subseteq E_{\text{avg}}$, as proven above. The error was in the interpretation of the topological chain: a finer topology yields a smaller closure, hence a smaller equilibrium set. This correction is important because it changes the interpretation of the hierarchy: the strictest equilibrium condition is the one in the join topology, not the average topology.

**Remark 5.5.6 (Interpretation).** The hierarchy captures three degrees of equilibrium:

- $E_\vee$ requires simultaneous forward and backward closeness, the strictest condition.
- $E_{\text{avg}}$ requires average closeness, a weaker condition.
- $E_\wedge$ requires only that the point is approximated by a set that is *both* forward- and backward-close, the weakest condition.

The hierarchy $E_\vee \subseteq E_{\text{avg}} \subseteq E_\wedge$ reflects the increasing permissiveness of the three conditions.

## 5.6 Local asymmetry and symmetric points

**Definition 5.6.1 (Local asymmetry at a point).** The *local asymmetry* of $d_\gamma$ at $b$ is
$$\alpha_\gamma(b) = \limsup_{b' \to_{\tau_{\text{avg}}} b} \frac{|d_\gamma(b, b') - d_\gamma(b', b)|}{d_{\text{avg}}(b, b')}.$$

**Proposition 5.6.2 (Range of local asymmetry).** $0 \le \alpha_\gamma(b) \le 2$ for all $b$.

*Proof.* For any $b \neq b'$, $|d_\gamma(b, b') - d_\gamma(b', b)| \le d_\gamma(b, b') + d_\gamma(b', b) = 2 d_{\text{avg}}(b, b')$. The ratio is therefore at most $2$. The lower bound is trivial. $\square$

**Definition 5.6.3 (Locally symmetric point).** $b$ is *locally symmetric* if $\alpha_\gamma(b) = 0$. The set of locally symmetric points is denoted $\operatorname{Sym}_\gamma(\mathcal{B})$.

**Proposition 5.6.4 (Symmetry points are closed).** $\operatorname{Sym}_\gamma(\mathcal{B})$ is closed in $\tau_{\text{avg}}$.

*Proof.* The map $(b, b') \mapsto |d_\gamma(b, b') - d_\gamma(b', b)| / d_{\text{avg}}(b, b')$ is continuous on $\{(b, b') : b \neq b'\}$ with respect to the product topology on $\mathcal{B} \times \mathcal{B}$ minus the diagonal. The set where the $\limsup$ vanishes is closed. $\square$

**Proposition 5.6.5 (Global symmetry implies local symmetry).** If $d_\gamma$ is symmetric on all of $\mathcal{B}$, then $\operatorname{Sym}_\gamma(\mathcal{B}) = \mathcal{B}$.

*Proof.* If $d_\gamma(b, b') = d_\gamma(b', b)$ for all $b, b'$, the numerator vanishes identically. $\square$

**Remark 5.6.6 (The converse is false).** A point can be locally symmetric without the metric being globally symmetric. The set $\operatorname{Sym}_\gamma(\mathcal{B})$ is the locus where the metric is *approaching* symmetry in the limit, which is a weaker condition than global symmetry. This distinction will matter in the equilibrium theory of Part IV.

## 5.7 Fixed points in the bitopological structure

**Definition 5.7.1 (Fixed point).** A state $b^* \in \mathcal{B}$ is a *fixed point* of the system if $Q(b^*, b^*) = 0$ and, for all $b' \in \mathcal{B}$,
$$\lim_{b \to b^*} Q(b, b') = Q(b^*, b') \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{b \to b^*} Q(b', b) = Q(b', b^*),$$
both limits taken in $\tau_{\text{avg}}$.

**Proposition 5.7.2 (Fixed points are in all equilibrium sets).** If $b^*$ is a fixed point, then $b^* \in E_\vee \cap E_{\text{avg}} \cap E_\wedge \cap E_\rightarrow \cap E_\leftarrow$.

*Proof.* At a fixed point, all $d_\gamma$-balls of positive radius contain $b^*$, so $b^*$ is in the closure of $U_{\text{pre}}$ in every topology. $\square$

**Remark 5.7.3 (The converses are open).** Proposition 5.7.2 is the trivial inclusion: fixed points are equilibrium points. The interesting questions are the converses: when is an equilibrium point a fixed point? When does $E_\vee = E_{\text{avg}} = E_\wedge$? These are open and are addressed in Part IV and Part V.

## 5.8 Summary

The asymmetry of the complex quasi-metric generates two topologies on $\mathcal{B}$: the forward topology $\tau_+$ and the backward topology $\tau_-$. The pair $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_+, \tau_-)$ is a bitopological space.

**The topological chain (Proposition 5.4.6):**
$$\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- \subseteq \tau_{\text{avg}} \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-.$$

**The equilibrium hierarchy (Theorem 5.5.3):**
$$E_\vee \subseteq E_{\text{avg}} \subseteq E_\wedge, \qquad E_\vee \subseteq E_\rightarrow \cap E_\leftarrow.$$

The join topology gives the strictest equilibrium condition (smallest set); the meet topology gives the weakest (largest set). The direction of inclusions is the reverse of what a naïve reading might suggest, because finer topologies give smaller closures.

**Local asymmetry (Proposition 5.6.2):** bounded by $2$ and vanishes at locally symmetric points. The set $\operatorname{Sym}_\gamma(\mathcal{B})$ is closed in $\tau_{\text{avg}}$ and contains all globally symmetric points.

**Fixed points (Proposition 5.7.2):** every fixed point is in every equilibrium set. The converses are open.

This chapter establishes the framework's distinctive geometry: the bitopological structure that arises from asymmetry, and the hierarchy of equilibrium concepts it generates. The next chapter completes Part II by developing the equilibrium structure in more detail.
